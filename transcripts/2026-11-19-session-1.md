## Ideas

**1. "Témoin": tamper-evident evidence recorder**
The iPhone records photos, audio or sensor events. Each chunk is hashed, chained to the previous one and signed with a Secure Enclave key, then streamed to the Mac. The Mac checks the chain and flags any gap or edit.
- *Demo:* change one byte of a stored file live, and the Mac shows exactly where the chain breaks.
- *Why it scores well:* it directly addresses Tampering and Repudiation from STRIDE, and the signing is a natural use of the Secure Enclave.

**2. Evil-maid detector**
You leave your Mac in a hotel room with the iPhone propped up watching it. Vision plus motion detection notices someone touching the laptop. The iPhone keeps a signed log and an encrypted snapshot, and the Mac checks it when you come back.
- *Why it's good:* the evil-maid attack is a well-known threat in cybersecurity, which gives your threat model a clear story.
- *Watch out for:* RGPD (GDPR). Do the processing on the device and store only events, not raw video.

**3. Offline signer over QR codes**
The iPhone never touches the network. The Mac shows a QR code with the hash of a document or transaction. The iPhone scans it, shows what it's about to sign, asks for Face ID, signs, and shows the signature back as a QR code.
- *Why it's strong:* with no network at all, the attack surface is tiny. Most of the STRIDE network threats simply go away, and you can argue that in the oral.
- *Bonus:* it shows you understand the "what you see is what you sign" problem.

**4. Shoulder-surfing guard**
The iPhone sits behind you on your desk as a rear camera. When a second face is looking at your screen, the Mac blurs or locks it.
- *Why it's good:* it's very visual and easy to demo.
- *Watch out for:* this is a bit thin on crypto, so you'd need to make the channel and the pairing really solid.

**5. Proximity key with relay detection**
This builds on the slides' "security key" idea. A vault on the Mac unlocks only when the paired iPhone is close by over BLE and answers a Face ID challenge. When you walk away, it locks.
- *The interesting part:* relay attacks. Try to detect them with tight response-time windows and RSSI checks.
- *Why it's good:* the cross-audit group will almost certainly try a relay attack, which makes for a great audit story.
- *Caution:* unlocking a vault inside your own app is fine. Unlocking the real macOS login screen is not realistically doable.

**6. Duress mode (an add-on for any vault or alarm)**
A special "duress" PIN or gesture opens a decoy state and silently sends a signed alert to the Mac. It's a nice extra feature on top of any of the ideas above.

Good idea. It's a stronger pitch if you start from a real problem than from a feature. Below are real iOS/macOS security pain points that fit your constraints (free account, iPhone as sensor, Mac as hub, local network only).

## Pain points and possible solutions

**1. Developer secrets sit in plain files on the Mac**
- **The problem:** SSH keys in `~/.ssh` and cloud credentials in `~/.aws` are often unencrypted on disk. Malicious npm and PyPI packages specifically search for these files and steal them. This happens often in real supply-chain attacks.
- **The solution:** use the iPhone as a hardware SSH key. The private key is created in the Secure Enclave and never leaves it. A small agent on the Mac speaks the standard `ssh-agent` protocol and passes each signing request to the iPhone. The iPhone shows which host is asking, you approve with Face ID, and it returns the signature.
- **Why it fits:** OpenSSH supports `ecdsa-sha2-nistp256`, which is exactly the P-256 key type the Secure Enclave can produce. `ssh`, `git push` and SSH-signed git commits would all work without changes.
- **Prior art:** Krypton did this but has been discontinued, and Secretive keeps keys in the Mac's own Secure Enclave, not on a separate device. You'd be filling a gap.

**2. Mac persistence is easy to miss**
- **The problem:** malware survives reboots by adding LaunchAgents, login items, SSH `authorized_keys` entries, `/etc/hosts` changes or configuration profiles. macOS does show a "background item added" notice, but people dismiss it. If the Mac is compromised, the malware can also hide warnings shown on that same Mac.
- **The solution:** the Mac agent hashes these locations and sends signed snapshots to the iPhone. The iPhone keeps the trusted baseline. Any change has to be approved with Face ID on the phone, so malware on the Mac can't approve its own changes. This is a classic integrity monitor where the checker lives on a separate device.
- **Honest limit:** malware with root access can make the agent lie. The mitigation is to fail secure: if the iPhone stops receiving signed heartbeats, it raises an alert.

**3. Passwords leak through the clipboard**
- **The problem:** copied passwords and 2FA codes end up in clipboard history. Clipboard managers on the Mac often store that history in plain text, and other apps can read the clipboard.
- **The solution:** the iPhone vault sends a credential over the encrypted channel, and the Mac helper types it straight into the focused field as keystrokes. The secret never touches the clipboard.
- **Honest limit:** this needs macOS Accessibility permission, and that permission is itself a security risk you'd have to cover in your threat model.

**4. Laptops get stolen while they're unlocked**
- **The problem:** in a café, the thief grabs the laptop while you're logged in and gets your whole open session. Android has a feature that detects this kind of theft and locks the phone; Macs have nothing like it.
- **The solution:** auto-lock the Mac when your iPhone (in your pocket) loses its link with it.
- **How you'd differ from existing tools:** apps like BLEUnlock and Near Lock rely on Bluetooth signals that can be faked. Yours would prove presence with signed, rotating messages from the iPhone and resist relay attacks. Locking the screen immediately is easy to do on macOS.

**5. Sensitive screenshots pile up in the photo library**
- **The problem:** people screenshot recovery phrases, passwords, IDs and bank details, and those photos often sync to iCloud.
- **The solution:** scan the library on the device with Vision text recognition, flag the risky images, and move them into an encrypted vault.
- **The catch:** the Mac hub doesn't add much here, so this fits the required architecture poorly.

**6. People approve security prompts without reading them**
- **The problem:** fake tech-support calls get people to install remote-access tools, and "Allow"-style prompts get accepted automatically out of habit.
- **The solution:** sensitive actions on the Mac need approval on the iPhone, with clear context about what's being approved.
- **The catch:** this is close to the SentryKey idea, but vaguer.

You're right that friction is a real cost. If every `git push` means picking up your phone, people will turn the feature off, and that's a security failure too. The fix is to make approvals **rare**, not to drop the idea.

## Which ideas are actually high-friction

| Idea | Day-to-day UX |
|---|---|
| Snatch lock | **Zero-touch.** You just walk away |
| Integrity monitor | **Almost zero-touch.** You only get a prompt when something changes, which should be rare |
| Screenshot scanner | **Zero-touch.** It runs in the background |
| SSH key | **High friction** if every use needs Face ID |
| Secure typing | **Medium.** But you'd be reaching for a password anyway |
| Context-aware approval | **High friction**, since it's prompts all the way down |

So half the list is already passive. The SSH key, my top pick, is the one with the UX problem.

## How to make the SSH key nearly invisible

**1. Approve once per session.**
One Face ID unlocks signing for, say, an hour, or until your phone leaves the Mac's range. YubiKeys and `ssh-agent` timeouts work the same way, and developers accept that.

**2. Approve automatically when your phone is nearby.**
If the iPhone proves it's close by (signed rotating Bluetooth messages, from the snatch-lock idea), routine signatures go through without any tap. That's the same pattern as unlocking a Mac with an Apple Watch.

**3. Set policies by risk.**
- Pushing to GitHub: approved automatically.
- Connecting to a production server: always needs Face ID.
- More than N signatures per minute: blocked, and the phone alerts you.

**4. Only interrupt on something unusual.**
A new host, an odd hour, or a burst of requests triggers a prompt. Everything else just gets recorded in a signed log you can review on the phone.

## The tradeoff to own in your threat model
Automatic approval brings back the risk you were removing: **malware on the Mac can request signatures silently while your phone is nearby**. It still can't steal the key, but it can use it. Your mitigations are per-host policies, rate limits and the visible log.

This tradeoff is actually a strength for the project. "We balanced security against friction, and here's exactly where we drew the line" is the kind of reasoning that does well in an oral defense.

## The reframed pitch
**"Zero-touch by default, prompt only when something looks wrong."** That merges the SSH key, the snatch lock and the policy engine into one product. For everyday use, the phone in your pocket is the key. You only reach for it when something is actually suspicious.

Does that direction work for you, or would you rather pick one of the fully passive ideas (snatch lock or integrity monitor) as the main project?

## What the vault fix adds

**When the vault is created (on the Mac, once):**
1. The Mac generates a temporary key pair and does a key exchange (ECDH) with the iPhone's public key.
2. HKDF turns the result into a wrapping key, which encrypts the vault key.
3. The Mac stores only the temporary public key and the wrapped vault key, then throws away everything else.

**When the vault is unlocked:**
1. The Mac sends the temporary public key to the iPhone.
2. The iPhone asks for Face ID and does the same key exchange inside the Secure Enclave.
3. The iPhone sends the shared result back over the encrypted channel.
4. The Mac rebuilds the wrapping key, unwraps the vault key and decrypts the vault.

In practice, that's:
- A second Secure Enclave key, for key agreement in addition to the signing key. That's one line in CryptoKit.
- One new message type, "unwrap request".
- A small wrap/unwrap function on the Mac.

## Two things that add design work, not code
- **A lost or reset phone means a lost vault**, because the key exists only in that iPhone's Secure Enclave. You'll need a recovery code generated at pairing (printed or written down). This is a little more work, but it's also a good point for the threat model.
- **After unlocking, the vault key sits in the Mac's memory.** Malware running at that exact moment could grab it. The fix protects the vault when it's locked, not while it's open. That's normal for any vault; just say so explicitly.

## Compared with switching to SSH
The two are about the same effort overall, with different kinds of work:

| | Vault + fix | SSH key |
|---|---|---|
| Extra crypto | Key agreement + wrapping (small) | None beyond signing |
| Extra plumbing | Vault file encryption, recovery code | The ssh-agent protocol (a binary format) and converting signatures to SSH's format, roughly a few hundred lines |
| Secret left on the Mac | Only while the vault is open | None, ever |
| Demo | Open a vault | Real `git push` / `ssh` |

So the fix is cheap enough that it shouldn't decide between the two. Pick based on which demo and pitch your group prefers. I'd still lean toward SSH, because it protects something people actually use and there's never a secret on the Mac.

**C1. "Which host is asking" cannot be known, and the Mac can fake it.** A SIGN_REQUEST contains only three fields: the key blob, the data and the flags. The data is the USERAUTH_REQUEST: a session ID (an opaque KEX hash), the username, the method and the key. It contains no host name. In step 2 the Mac agent "wraps it with the host", so the host label is whatever the Mac says, and malware on the Mac can say anything. That makes per-host policy and the "what you see is what you sign" claim invalid as written.
- **Attack:** malware sends its own SIGN_REQUEST and labels it "github.com", a known host. The phone auto-approves.
- **Fix:**
  - Have the Mac forward the `session-bind@openssh.com` message (host key, session ID, server signature over the session ID; OpenSSH 8.9+).
  - The phone checks that server signature itself, and checks that the session ID inside the signed data equals the bound one.
  - Policy is then keyed on the verified **host-key fingerprint**. The phone keeps its own TOFU mapping from fingerprint to a name. The Mac's known_hosts is not trusted.
  - Requests with no binding, or with data that is not USERAUTH or SSHSIG, get Face ID or a deny.
  - Without this check the phone is a signing oracle for arbitrary data. Parse the data strictly and show the username, plus the namespace for git SSHSIG.
- **Residual risk to document:** OpenSSH's restriction feature "does not address" malicious local clients (agent-restrict.html). Malware can still open a genuine session to any host whose key the phone trusts.
- Sources: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openssh/openssh-portable/master/PROTOCOL.agent, https://www.openssh.org/agent-restrict.html, https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-sshm-ssh-agent/ (the datatracker page reported it as published as RFC 9987; I did not confirm that independently).

**C2. Zero-touch signing contradicts `biometryCurrentSet` and data protection.**
- A Secure Enclave key whose SecAccessControl includes `.biometryCurrentSet` needs biometrics every time it is used (reuse of an authentication context is capped at a few minutes; unverified). Auto-approve is therefore impossible with that key.
- If the key is not gated, the only control is the app's policy code.
- A phone locked in a pocket cannot use `WhenUnlocked` keys, and `NSFileProtectionComplete` stops the background audit-log append.
- **Fix:** choose explicitly and document it.
  - Option A: two keys, one gated and one ungated, each listed separately in authorized_keys per host tier.
  - Option B: accept app-level gating.
  - Use `AfterFirstUnlockThisDeviceOnly` and `completeUntilFirstUserAuthentication` for the log, and justify it.
- Source: unverified. Check the Apple Platform Security guide on Keychain data protection.

### HIGH

**H1. "Proven nearby" proves nothing against the main threat.** Malware on the Mac sits next to the phone, so it needs no relay. Proximity only helps when the Mac is stolen or remote. A BLE link or RSSI reading can be relayed, and BLE has no distance bounding.
- **Fix:** present proximity as a usability feature, not a security control. Add per-host and global rate limits, Face ID on the first use of each host key, a short session time-to-live tied to the Mac screen being unlocked, a visible signature counter, and Face ID for SSHSIG commit signing.
- Source: unverified.

**H2. The pairing fingerprint is not specified.** If it is just the Mac's key, comparing it adds nothing, because the QR already authenticates the Mac to the phone. The phone's public key then travels back over BLE with no authentication, so a man-in-the-middle can swap it.
- **Fix:** derive the comparison code from both public keys and a nonce (a short authentication string). Better still, put a one-time secret in the QR and have the phone authenticate its key with that secret; this removes the human comparison step, which users click through. State the assumption that the Mac is not compromised at pairing time.
- Source: unverified.

**H3. The channel has no forward secrecy, no key separation and no stated binding.**
- **Fix:**
  - Use an ephemeral ECDH per session, signed by both long-term keys (a SIGMA-style handshake).
  - Use a separate key-agreement identity key in the Secure Enclave. Never reuse the SSH signing key.
  - Use an HKDF salt built from both session nonces, and an `info` that covers the protocol version, both identity fingerprints and a per-direction label. This gives two keys, one per direction.
  - Use 96-bit counter nonces and abort when a counter wraps.
  - Make the counter the anti-replay mechanism, and use the timestamp only as a coarse ±window, because the two devices' clocks can drift apart.
  - The brief says "every message is signed". AES-GCM authenticates but does not sign, so justify this in the doc.
- Source: unverified.

**H4. Losing the phone or re-enrolling Face ID means lockout.** A `biometryCurrentSet` key is destroyed when Face ID is re-enrolled. Deleting the app, or the 7-day free-account expiry followed by a reinstall, may also wipe it (unverified).
- **Fix:** make a backup key mandatory, stored offline or on a second device. Ship an authorized_keys inventory and a revocation runbook.

### MEDIUM

**M1. The audit log can be deleted or truncated.** A hash chain does not detect a cut-off tail or a wiped log, and the doc never says who signs the log.
- **Fix:** sign with a separate Secure Enclave key. Anchor the latest chain head and counter on the Mac, and have each side check the other on reconnect.
- **Cheap win for the other team:** CLAUDE.md forbids host names in logs, yet the audit log records them. Encrypt the log and use fingerprints instead of names.

**M2. MFA fatigue and BLE flooding.** The "Ask" path invites prompt spam, and the notification text comes from the Mac.
- **Fix:** cap prompts per minute, apply a cooldown that denies by default, show only the context the phone verified itself, and add a "report as not me" action that blocks the Mac.

**M3. The signature format conversion is undocumented.**
- RFC 5656 §3.1.2 uses SHA-256 for nistp256, with the signature blob `string "ecdsa-sha2-nistp256"`, then `string(mpint r ‖ mpint s)`.
- CryptoKit's `rawRepresentation` is r‖s, 32 bytes each, and `signature(for: Data)` hashes with SHA-256 internally (unverified). Do not pre-hash and then call it, or the data gets hashed twice.
- mpint rules: strip leading 0x00 bytes, prepend 0x00 when the high bit is set, and encode zero as an empty string.
- Public key format: `string "ecdsa-sha2-nistp256"`, `string "nistp256"`, `string` of the 65-byte x963 point.
- Test against `ssh-keygen -Y verify`.
- Source: https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5656#section-3.1.2

### LOW

- A fixed BLE advertising identifier lets anyone track the phone. Use rotating identifiers.
- Size limits for sign requests are not specified.
- The policy rules and what a "session" means are undefined (listed as open questions). The other team will flag every one of them.
- There is no STRIDE or MASVS mapping. The relevant controls are CRYPTO-1/2, AUTH-2/3, NETWORK-1, STORAGE-1 and PLATFORM-1 (my mapping, see above).

### Sound choices

- A non-exportable Secure Enclave key, using P-256, the only curve the Secure Enclave offers.
- The standard ssh-agent protocol, with no changes to ssh.
- Encryption at the application layer, instead of relying on BLE pairing.
- A QR code as the out-of-band pairing channel.
- Shared protocol code that is reviewed and tested once.
- No third-party dependencies, CryptoKit only.
- An honest statement in CLAUDE.md that malware can use the key but not steal it; it should be copied into ARCHITECTURE.md.

## Claude

You probably mean the **Secure Enclave**. It's a separate security chip inside the iPhone's processor (the A16 in your 14 Pro), isolated from the rest of the system.

## What it does
- **Generates keys and keeps them locked inside.** A private key created in the Secure Enclave can never be read or exported, not by your app, not by iOS, not even by Apple.
- **Uses keys on request.** Your app asks it to sign or decrypt something, it does the operation internally and returns only the result.
- **Handles Face ID.** The face data is stored and matched inside the Secure Enclave. Your app never sees it, it only gets a yes or no.
- **Ties keys to conditions.** A key can be set to work only after Face ID, only when the phone is unlocked, only on this device, and so on. The chip enforces this itself, so even a bug in your app can't get around it.

## The Keychain, which people often confuse with it
The **Keychain** is iOS's encrypted storage for small secrets: passwords, tokens, keys.
- The data is encrypted with keys protected by the Secure Enclave.
- But when your app reads a Keychain item, the secret is **decrypted and handed to your app**, so it's in memory.
- A Secure Enclave key, by contrast, **never leaves the chip**. It doesn't exist anywhere else.

## Limits to know for your project
- **One key type only: P-256** (elliptic curve), for signing (ECDSA) and key exchange (ECDH). No RSA, no Ed25519.
- **You can't import a key**; it has to be created inside the chip.
- **If the phone is lost or Face ID is re-enrolled**, the keys gated on Face ID are gone. That's why the design needs a backup key.

## Why it matters for SentryKey
It's the whole point of the project: the passkey-style key lives in the Secure Enclave, so even if the iPhone app or the Mac is compromised, the key itself can't be stolen. In Swift, you use it through CryptoKit's `SecureEnclave.P256.Signing.PrivateKey`.

Your MacBook Air also has a Secure Enclave if it's Apple silicon, so the Mac's own identity key can be non-exportable too.

- **iCloud Keychain does sync some items** between your Apple devices: saved passwords, Wi-Fi networks, passkeys made by Safari. An app can mark its own items as "synchronizable" too.
- **Secure Enclave keys never sync.** They're generated inside one device's chip and can't leave it. Your iPhone's key exists only in the iPhone.
- **Items marked `ThisDeviceOnly` never sync either.** That's what `CLAUDE.md` requires.
- **With your free account, app-level sync likely isn't usable anyway.** It relies on iCloud, which the brief says is reserved for paid accounts. I'm not 100% sure the Keychain part is blocked, but the brief excludes iCloud, so don't design around it.

## Why you don't want it shared
The whole security of SentryKey comes from the iPhone holding something the Mac doesn't have.
- **If the same secret is on both devices,** malware on the Mac can read it from the Mac's copy. The iPhone and Face ID become decoration.
- **With a key only in the iPhone's Secure Enclave,** malware on the Mac can't do anything without the phone and your face.
- **Syncing would also widen the attack surface:** anyone who takes over your Apple ID could get the secret on a device they control.

## What each device keeps
| Device | Keeps |
|---|---|
| **iPhone** | Its private keys (Secure Enclave, never leave), plus the Mac's *public* key |
| **Mac** | Its own private key (in its Secure Enclave if Apple silicon), plus the iPhone's *public* keys (Keychain, `ThisDeviceOnly`) |

They only ever share **public** keys, exchanged once at pairing through the QR code. Public keys aren't secret, so it doesn't matter that both sides have them. What matters is that nobody can swap them, and that's what the QR pairing and the Keychain storage protect.

That brings up a question the jury will almost certainly ask: **"Why use the iPhone at all, when the Mac could do it with Touch ID?"** A free tool, Secretive, already does exactly that for SSH. You need a solid answer.

## What the Mac's Secure Enclave alone protects
- **Key theft:** malware can't extract the key, the same as on the iPhone.
- **Silent use:** if the key requires Touch ID, malware can't sign without you touching the sensor.

## What it doesn't protect, and where the iPhone adds value
1. **A display you can trust.** If the Mac is compromised, everything on its screen can be faked. Malware can trigger a Touch ID prompt at the moment you're expecting one, for example right after you type `git push`, and use your touch to sign something else. The iPhone is a **separate device with its own screen**: what it shows comes from verified data, not from the infected Mac. Security keys like YubiKeys exist for this same reason, so a compromised computer can't trick you.
2. **Separation of devices.** Compromising the Mac doesn't compromise the phone. To get a signature, an attacker would need both devices plus your face. That's the point of the architecture the brief imposes.
3. **One key for several Macs.** The iPhone works as your key on any Mac you pair it with: work, home, a lab machine. A key in the Mac's Secure Enclave is stuck on that one Mac.
4. **Macs without Touch ID:** a Mac mini, a desktop, or a laptop used with its lid closed on an external display.

Point 1 only fully holds if the phone verifies what it shows. For SSH, that's the `session-bind` check from the security review: the phone confirms the server's identity itself instead of trusting the Mac.

## How to use the Mac's Secure Enclave in the project
Put the **Mac's identity key** (the one used for pairing and the channel) in the Mac's Secure Enclave. Then neither device's private key can ever be stolen, which makes a nice symmetry to present.
